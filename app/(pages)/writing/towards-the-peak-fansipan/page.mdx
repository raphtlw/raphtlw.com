- people who are looking to start travelling, unsure of where to go

In order to hike to Phang Xi Pang, there are 3 trails that lead you to the summit:

### Tram Ton Trail

The Tram Ton trail starts near Vietnam's highest mountain pass, Tram Ton Pass (also called Heaven's Gate), at about 1,850 meters above sea level. This is the most commonly used and accessible trail, especially for guided hikes. It's around 11 kilometers to the summit, with a mix of gradual and undulating sections, especially near the end. Most groups do it over two days with a night camp at 2,800 meters, though experienced hikers can complete it in one. This is the trail we and many others used for our descent from Fansipan.

### Sin Chai Trail

Beginning at the Hmong village of Sin Chai (1,380 meters), this is the shortest but steepest and most rugged route. It's the least used and parts of the trail are heavily overgrown, making it easy to lose the path. Though challenging, it doesn't require ropes or special climbing gear. It's roughly 9 kilometers long and known for being the most difficult and wild.

### Cat Cat Trail

This trail starts from Cat Cat village near Sapa, at about 1,300 meters elevation, and is the longest route to the summit. While it's also steep and demanding, it's considered safer than the Sin Chai trail. However, there's limited information available and it's less frequently hiked.

Our starting point was from Hoang Lien National Park, which starts us from an altitude of ~1,800 meters.

We were all happy and excited to start the hike, fearless, while also feeling a little nervous for what was about to come.

The rocks were not as slippery as Mount Rinjani, which meant the way up was easier as we got closer to the top.

The problem with Mount Fansipan is while the altitude difference is little, the total elevation gain is a lot higher than 3000m because the route upwards isn't just _upwards_. It's a long spaghetti of windy ups and downs. So maybe you have climbed for an hour but you may just only gain 100m in elevation. I wouldn't say it's an encouraging route, at all.

The temperature was still alright (16 degrees) at the national park so we didn't feel the need to wear a jacket, in fact we felt hot while making the ascend.

Mount Fansipan isn't a smooth upwards climb. There are tons of different sized rocks, and slippery moss which might make it harder to climb. Along the side of the way up you also see streams of spring water flowing throughout coming from the crevices of the many fissure springs.

The difference between Rinjani and Fansipan is, the level and amount of slopes going up and down. There are more of these I encountered when climbing Fansipan.

There are no toilets along the way so if you need to relieve yourself you need to use the bushes.

I used the time we took to rest to put on my sunglasses and neck warmer. The neck warmer will help you the most when you feel cold, because that's where blood flows the most throughout the body.

During the hike, our guide introduced himself as Minh, a Hmong speaking person who is part of the Miao ethnic group, the largest ethnic minority group in China.

The H'Mong are the largest ethnic minority group in Hoang Lien National Park. You will also encounter other ethnic communities such as the Dao and Day. Starting a conversation with them is often easy because many of them speak English well. They have learned different languages through interactions with tourists or from school. By getting to know them, you may hear fascinating stories, enjoy a shared meal, and experience genuine hospitality if they invite you into their home. Even spending just one day with them can offer a memorable and meaningful cultural experience.

Together with him as a porter was his uncle, also from the same ethnic tribe as himself.

We were handed a hiking stick for each of the three of us and each a large bottle of water.

Along the way up, we saw water buffalos, wild vegetables growing on the raw soil of the national park, and the amazing streams of spring water flowing down from the many fissure springs.

They both kept speaking to each other in some sort of dialect. At first I thought it was just Vietnamese spoken a different way, but it turns out as I talked to him more he explained in Vietnam there are 54 different tribes, and his family migrated from China when he was 4.

He learned the Hmong language from his parents (like teaching simple words like eat dinner, etc etc) first before spending 12 years learning the Vietnamese language in school.

The ethnic locals in Vietnam have their own language but they learn the national language so they can speak to each other with a common language.

On the way up we saw the cable car, which the guide explained its construction was completed in 2013 and takes only about 20 minutes from Fansipan to Sapa Central

Every day the cable car runs from 7:30 AM to 3:30 PM.

Along the way up we saw other guides part of the group which maintains the hiking trips for tourists. They all keep in touch using their phones, calling each other when necessary.

We approached the first base camp at 12:45 PM and ate lunch there.
