# Towards the Peak: Mount Fansipan
16 July 2025

When I first sat down to write this, I admit I was unsure where to begin. My hike up Mount Fansipan this past March wasn't some great epiphany; it wasn’t heroic or life-changing. But it mattered. After all the sweat, sore legs, and laughter, the mountain is still in my mind days later.

If you’re reading this, you might be:
- Thinking about visiting Sapa, and debating if Fansipan is worth the effort
- Packing for your own climb, hunting for others’ real experiences
- Or maybe, like me now, you’ve gone already and are trying to make sense of what it meant

This is not a guidebook. It’s a conversation. I just hope it will be as useful as it is honest—and if something here helps, feel free to reach out.

## Tips if you ever visit Hanoi

**SIM cards:**
Just after landing in Hanoi, I sorted a local SIM card for about $10 USD—sometimes you’ll see offers for “unlimited GB” for peanuts, but if it feels “too cheap” or they don’t ask for your passport, be wary. I got mine at an official counter at the airport and confirmed activation by dialing the operator’s check number.

**Cash:**
Withdraw cash at the airport—BIDV, Vietinbank, Techcombank, and more are available at both Domestic (T1) and International (T2). The main tips?
- BIDV charges about 4.2% fee, 60K dong minimum for withdrawals at airports
- Regular banks (in town) are slightly cheaper (4%, 55K min)
- Revolut worked for me, but you must pay a fee after your free limit

**Food:**
Don’t expect much at the airport, sadly. The banh mi I ate was underwhelming—but hey, you’re not in Vietnam yet for the food, you’re in the transit zone.

## The Not-so-Glorious Sleeper Bus

The true introduction to Vietnam came on the bus to Sapa.
We waited half an hour at Noi Bai for our driver—tour operator details changed so much I lost track: eventually, it was “Sapa Open Tour” (avoid if you can).

I’d love to romanticize the sleeper bus, but I’d be lying. Picture:
- Claustrophobic bunks, too short to sit up, too cramped to stretch out
- Every surface shakes as the bus bounces through mountain switchbacks
- The staff and drivers shouting conversations at full volume across the aisle
- Wi-Fi and charging ports that simply don’t work

For four hours I tried and failed to rest, doze, or even work on my laptop. My seat/mattress felt like a playpen for grown humans, and I found myself longing for a “seater” rather than a sleeper.
If you’re planning this trip: book a regular seat bus when possible. On the way down I did, and the difference was night and day.

As the miles passed, the fog outside became cyclonic and pale. At one point we skirted the Vietnamese-Chinese border near Lau Cai—close enough to see border stations and feel a subtle excitement that borders always give.

## Sapa: Fog, Cold, and French-Chinese Charm

Check-in at Sapa Yen Hotel was like stepping into a parallel world—elegant, fresh rooms, a mountain chill (cold as “aircon everywhere”), and a view fringed with pine and mist.

If you walk around Sapa at dusk:
You’ll see streetlights shining through thick clouds, kids in colorful jackets selling trinkets, and groups of H’Mong, Dao, and Tay ethnic people. Their children sell little woven bracelets or beg for coins—sometimes for education, sometimes not.

The city had more tourists than locals, and English is only spoken by those working daily with foreigners. Still, the colonial French architecture mixed with Chinese shophouse chaos was mesmerizing.

**Pro tip:**
If you’re looking for food, be discerning but open-minded. One dinner we enjoyed was a steaming hotpot overflowing with fresh mountain beef (so fresh you could eat it raw with just lime and salt). Vegetables, especially bamboo shoots, tasted cleaner and earthier than anything at home. The higher the altitude, the lighter the flavor—don’t be shy about adding a pinch of salt!

Perks of Sapa at night?
- Coffee culture is strong (we found communist-style cafes, cozy and smoky)
- Cheap soju—about 70,000 dong, or less than $3 SGD
- Feeling of sudden freedom: cold air stinging your skin, surrounded by fellow travelers and strangers alike, it feels like anything could happen

## The Climb Begins

We set out on Thursday, 27 March, after sunrise (the sun rises sharp and early here, about 6:00am).
Breakfast at the hotel: pancakes (no honey, just thick local chocolate sauce), pho, and strong black coffee. I pocketed a few instant coffee sachets for emergencies—good call, as it turned out!

Our guide Minh arrived at 9. Friendly, quiet, and intensely focused.
H’Mong by background, Minh is part of the Miao ethnic community—one of 54 official ethnic groups in Vietnam, originally from China. His English was self-taught (“from tourists, from YouTube, everywhere, you know?”). His uncle came as our porter.

We signed into Hoàng Liên National Park around 1,800 meters (starting altitude) and received hiking sticks and water bottles. The air was brisk, not freezing, but enough to keep my jacket on standby.

## About the Trails

Mount Fansipan can be reached by three main trails.
Here’s the real breakdown:

**1. Tram Ton Trail**
- The "regular" route, ~11km, starts from Heaven’s Gate/Tram Ton Pass (1,850m)
- Well-marked, mixture of stairs and wild track; most groups do it in two days, camping overnight at 2,800m, but ambitious hikers go faster

**2. Sin Chai Trail**
- Shortest (9km) but steepest and most wild; starts from Sin Chai village (1,380m)
- Rugged, overgrown, easy to lose, but no ropes needed; only for experienced hikers

**3. Cat Cat Trail**
- Longest, starts from Cat Cat village (1,300m); scenic and gradual, considered safer than Sin Chai but still demanding

We ascended via Cat Cat and descended on Tram Ton.
*Note: The route is never simply “up.” It turns, dips, and undulates like spaghetti on a plate. Your net elevation gain is 1,300–3,143m, but you’ll climb and re-climb terrain so many times, the *actual* elevation gain feels much, much higher—closer to 3,000 meters total!*

## Into the Mountain

We switched off our phones, and immediately time felt different. The forest took over—no city noise, no buzz, just bootsteps, birds, and the slap of water against mossy rock.

The route was unforgiving, but fair:
- Steep muddy hills, then sudden descents
- Moss-covered boulders, slippery and unpredictable
- Cool streams to cross (ice-cold water, crystal clear)
- Sun sometimes glaring through the trees, but most times, a thick mist

I was sweating after just 30 minutes. Hiking in the cold can be paradoxical: your hands feel cold, but your back and face feel hot. I quickly put on my neck warmer and sunglasses, stashed everything important in waterproof bags, and pressed on.

*(Tip: There are no toilets on the trail. Go before you leave, or be ready to be one with the bushes!)*

## The People: Minh and His World

Minh and his uncle kept up a running conversation in H’Mong—the kind of familial banter you don’t understand, but you know means home.
I asked about their language: Minh replied, “My people from China, from Miao-Zu. I learn to speak Vietnamese in school twelve years, but at home, only H’Mong."

The H’Mong, Dao, Day, and others live all throughout Hoàng Liên National Park. Conversation is easy if you’re friendly; most guides speak enough English or have picked it up along the way. Minh’s stories of village life, food, and how they help each other on the mountain made me aware of how deep the local network runs.

Every so often, phones would beep: guides checking in with base or updating family, signal sometimes stronger on the peaks than in Sapa town below.

## Lunch with a View

We reached our first “base camp” at 12:45 pm, at about 2,400 meters. Lunch was spread on a rock:
- Colored sticky rice, surprisingly fragrant
- Steamed fish cakes
- Slices of mango
- Lime and salt (essential for everything—especially with the sweat you shed!)

We drank from a spring using a metal cup: the coldest, freshest mineral water I’ve ever had.

## Higher Up: Camp 2 and Vietnamese Hospitality

As we gained altitude, the terrain changed.
The last push to 2,800 meters forced us up endless metal ladders and rough stone steps—at times, my legs felt like they were boiling inside frozen air.

Clouds moved in; visibility dropped. Minh’s uncle started preparing dinner outside, in the wind, over hot charcoals.
If I learned anything here, it’s that food tastes better the more tired and cold you are.

Dinner was steamboat:
- Local pork and mushrooms
- Wild bamboo shoots
- “Mystery greens” (I never did learn their names—basil, purple leaves, pungent herbs)
- Rice boiled in the broth
- Side shots of local rice wine (baijiu, 41–43%!)

Somewhere between steamy soup and that burning shot, a black cat climbed onto our shelf. By night, it had snuggled into my sleeping bag—sharing warmth on a freezing mountaintop. For a while, none of us minded.

## Summit Day: Up Before Dawn

We awoke at 4:30 am to total darkness and chill. Packing was wordless and slow: zip, click, step into boots, check the headlamp, deep breath.

The summit route is... not gentle. Ladders and steep rocky stairs—your legs will ache, your breath will burn in the icy air, and yet you keep moving.

By the upper slope, mist shifted and parted for moments. We passed a massive Buddha statue (bronze and silent in the mountain light), then pushed forward, step by dizzy step.

At 6:00 am we arrived:
A gray, windswept triangle of metal marks the top of Vietnam, the top of Indochina—3143 meters. Cold seeping through gloves, the sky a flat sheet of cloud.

No drama at the summit. Just an odd, gentle silence as we all stood, smiling, tired, present. I opened the soju bottle I’d lugged up and took a sip—part ritual, part reward.

## The Way Down: Bamboo Rat and Back Again

Descent was slower, knees feeling every drop.
We took the Tram Ton route, famed for being the “easy” trail—but after two days climbing, no path feels easy.

About midway, Minh and his uncle stopped, peering intently into some undergrowth. A few minutes later they returned, faces triumphant, holding a blue plastic tub—with a huge bamboo rat inside! Apparently, a local delicacy (eaten for meat, sometimes prized for its blood, Minh told me, though I let that detail be).
The rat hissed at us, furious and wild.

The rest of the trail was, paradoxically, more peaceful. Mountain silence is different after the summit. Each shrine, waterfall, and ridgetop felt like a gentle return to earth.

## Back in Sapa: Showers, Soju, and Stillness

We retrieved our bags from Sapa Yen (left safely), then checked into My Gallery Boutique Hotel. After so many hours on foot, nothing feels better than a real shower and a hot meal—except, maybe, that feeling of walking out with slippers and laundry actually done.

We wandered town in a daze. Bought coffee, chatted, compared stories. Even now, days later, I find myself recalling mountain moments—meals, silences, that stubborn little cat—more than the “big” events.

## Post-climb Recovery & Notes

- **Post-climb care:** If you sunburn, treat it quickly.
- Clean your boots—things get muddy.
- Slippers and soap are a must for recovery.
- Local minimarts will bag your purchases for you (little touches you notice on tired feet).
- Your IG stories and Strava uploads are more for *you* than anyone else.

---

## Practical Details & Tips

- **Gear:** Waterproof hiking shoes, trekking poles, thermal base, gloves, headlamp, neck warmer, and spare dry clothes for nights are essential.
- **SIM:** Buy at airport, double-check passport registration.
- **ATM:** Withdraw enough at the airport; Revolut & others charge service fees.
- **Trail:** Ascent via Cat Cat or Sin Chai for challenge and scenery, descent via Tram Ton for ease.
- **Guide:** Book a local H’Mong guide (shoutout to Minh!).
- **Accommodations:** Sapa Yen Hotel, My Gallery Boutique Hotel—both reliable and friendly.

**Cable Car?**
Runs 7:30am–3:30pm daily if you want an “express” way up/down—20 minutes versus two days on foot.

---

## Final Thoughts

Fansipan didn’t give me a life-changing revelation. But as I walked down the last stretch, legs aching but content, I realized the real “peak” wasn’t the summit marker.

It was the space—between questions and answers, strangers and companions, silence and conversation—where presence itself became the reward.

If you’re planning to climb Mount Fansipan, or just remember what it felt like to be there, I hope you find what you came for. And if you need any specific advice, message me.
